Here is what we found. Finding one. Medicare spending per prescription fell by about two thirds for the ten negotiated drugs between twenty twenty-five and the first quarter of twenty twenty-six. All ten fell by at least thirty percent. The comparison groups barely moved, around one percent. So the negotiated prices clearly show up in what Medicare records.

But here is the catch. Those numbers are before rebates. Since these drugs already had rebates of almost half, the real savings for Medicare are much smaller than two thirds. One academic study projects a real saving of only about nine percent. So the honest headline is: the sticker side of the bill collapsed, the real saving is smaller, and we cannot measure it exactly with public data.

Finding two. On the very day the negotiated prices started, several companies cut their list prices. Eliquis by forty-three percent, Jardiance by forty-four, Farxiga by thirty-seven, and Fiasp by seventy-five. Januvia had already cut by forty-two percent a year earlier. Why would a company cut its sticker price? Because several penalties and discounts are tied to the list price. Bristol Myers told Reuters that the lower Eliquis price helps it avoid inflation penalties. And a rural health office in Pennsylvania reports a side effect: hospitals in the three-forty-B discount program used to buy Eliquis for about a penny, and from July they pay two hundred forty-five dollars a package.

How big are those rebates? For the first ten drugs Medicare negotiated, rebates took almost forty-five percent off the gross cost in twenty twenty-four. That is according to MedPAC, the commission that advises Congress on Medicare. Insulin is the famous example: between twenty twelve and twenty nineteen, sales of four leading insulins measured at list prices roughly doubled, while the money their manufacturers actually kept fell by about forty percent. Analysts call that the gross-to-net bubble.

So why do list prices matter if almost nobody pays them? Three reasons. One: patients. When a patient is in their deductible, or pays a percentage of the price, that percentage is calculated on the list-based price, not on the secret net price. Two: other government programs. Medicaid, the program for low-income people, gets a legal rebate calculated from the manufacturer's prices, plus an extra rebate when prices rise faster than inflation. And hospitals in a program called three-forty-B buy at a ceiling price linked to that same Medicaid formula. For drugs whose price rose a lot, that ceiling can drop to about a penny. Three: the middlemen. Historically, part of what P-B-Ms earn has been tied to list prices and to the size of rebates.

One more piece of background. Since twenty twenty-five, Medicare patients have a yearly cap on what they pay out of pocket for pharmacy drugs: two thousand dollars in twenty twenty-five, two thousand one hundred in twenty twenty-six. People on very expensive drugs hit that cap early in the year.

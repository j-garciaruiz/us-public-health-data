Now what we did and found. We used three free government sources: Medicare's quarterly spending per drug, which gives spending per prescription before rebates; Medicaid's weekly survey of what pharmacies pay, called nay-dack; and the official file of negotiated prices. We compared the ten drugs with the fifteen negotiated for twenty twenty-seven and with about seven hundred forty other brand-name drugs.

Finding one: comparing all of twenty twenty-five with the first three months of twenty twenty-six, Medicare's spending per prescription fell by about two thirds for the ten drugs, and every one fell at least thirty percent, while the comparison groups moved about one percent. That fits the new payment cap, though it does not prove cause. And it is before rebates. Since these drugs already had rebates of about forty-five percent, the real saving is much smaller. A study projects about nine percent lower real spending in twenty twenty-six.

Finding two: on the very day the negotiated prices started, several companies cut list prices, and some drugs that were not negotiated moved with them. Boehringer cut its Jardiance combination pills by the same forty-four percent on the same day.

Finding three: pharmacies still buy most of these drugs well above the negotiated price, two to three and a half times for Januvia, Xarelto, Entresto, Stelara and Enbrel. That is a gap between a price benchmark and the negotiated price, not a measured amount owed, but it shows why pharmacies depend on refunds. In a survey of over five hundred independent pharmacies, two thirds waited twenty-two days or more, and six in ten dipped into their savings.

As far as we can find, nobody has yet measured actual twenty twenty-six spending across all ten drugs. The closest study looks only at Stelara.

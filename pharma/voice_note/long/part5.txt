Who wins and who loses from a lower list price? It depends on contracts, so think of these as likely effects. Patients with private insurance who pay a percentage or are in their deductible can pay less, because their share usually follows the list price. People without insurance paying cash may pay less, if the pharmacy's cash price drops. Patients with a fixed copay usually see little change.

Employers and insurers may not save much, because the rebate they used to receive can shrink along with the list price. Their real cost could fall, stay the same, or even rise.

P-B-Ms can lose, where part of their income is a percentage of list prices or of rebates. And with lower list prices, manufacturers may have less room to buy good placement with rebates. A study of asthma inhalers found that after list price cuts in twenty twenty-four, use of generics jumped by twenty-three percentage points, possibly for that reason.

Medicaid's rebate can fall with the price, so Medicaid may not save much either. Hospitals in the three-forty-B program lose their penny prices: from July, the three-forty-B price of Eliquis went from about a penny to two hundred forty-five dollars a package. And the manufacturers? They give up list price revenue that was partly being rebated away anyway, and they can owe smaller rebates, refunds and penalties. Bristol Myers told Reuters its Eliquis cut helps it avoid Medicare inflation penalties.

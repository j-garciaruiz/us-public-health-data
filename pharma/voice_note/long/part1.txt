Let's begin with who is involved when a Medicare patient fills a prescription for a brand-name drug. The manufacturer makes the drug and publishes a list price. A wholesaler buys it and sells it to the pharmacy. The pharmacy hands it to the patient and gets paid by the patient's drug plan. That plan is a private insurer that Medicare pays to cover pharmacy drugs. This part of Medicare is called Part D. And behind the plan sits a pharmacy benefit manager, a P-B-M. The P-B-M runs the drug benefit for the plan: it decides which drugs are on the plan's list, it negotiates secret rebates with the manufacturers, and it sets how much pharmacies are paid. The three biggest P-B-Ms handle about eighty percent of American prescriptions, and they belong to the same groups that own big insurers.

Now the prices, which is where it gets confusing, because one drug has many prices. First, the list price, the sticker price the manufacturer publishes. Second, what the pharmacy pays the wholesaler, which for brand drugs is very close to that list price. Third, what the plan and the patient together pay the pharmacy, usually tied to list-based prices plus a small fee. And fourth, after the pill is dispensed, the manufacturer pays the P-B-M or the plan a rebate, a secret discount, in exchange for good placement on the plan's list. List price minus rebates is the net price, the real price, and it is confidential, drug by drug.

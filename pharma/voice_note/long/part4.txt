How long does a negotiated price last? It applies every year until Medicare finds that a generic or biosimilar competitor is really being sold. Then the drug leaves the program at the start of a later year; the approval of a competitor alone is not enough. Four of the first ten are already leaving on January first, twenty twenty-seven: Stelara, Entresto, Xarelto, and the insulin NovoLog with Fiasp. Meanwhile the price is adjusted for inflation every year. In the official file, Eliquis goes from two hundred thirty-one dollars for thirty days in twenty twenty-six to about two hundred thirty-seven in twenty twenty-seven. And Medicare can renegotiate a drug in a later round, as it is doing with Tradjenta.

How does the pharmacy actually get the negotiated price? The most the plan and the patient together can be charged is the negotiated price plus a dispensing fee. But the pharmacy may have bought the drug at close to the list price. So the manufacturer must make up the difference, usually as a refund after the fact; by default, the list price minus the negotiated price. A government system called the Medicare Transaction Facilitator passes the claim data along. Medicare says refunds arrive about twenty-one days after the prescription is filled, on average, but that is an average, not a promise.

Now your question about what a list price cut does for everyone else. Several companies cut list prices on January first: Eliquis by forty-three percent, Jardiance by forty-four, Farxiga by thirty-seven, and Fiasp by seventy-five. Januvia had cut forty-two percent a year earlier.

Finally, what the main papers say, briefly. On savings: Jeon and colleagues project about nine percent lower real spending in twenty twenty-six. Wouters and colleagues, in JAMA, find the first negotiated prices are eight to forty-two percent below the old net prices, but still above prices in six other rich countries, except insulin. For round two, Gabriel and colleagues find prices a median thirty-eight percent below net prices, still about double what other rich countries pay. Rome and Kesselheim, in the New England Journal, call round two a growing success: forty-four percent savings versus twenty-two percent in round one.

On patients: Castle and colleagues model average yearly savings of about six hundred eighty-five dollars in stand-alone plans and four hundred seventy in Medicare Advantage, but nothing extra for people already at the cap. Another study finds full coverage of the negotiated drugs in twenty twenty-six, with a little more step therapy for two of the diabetes drugs.

On launch prices: a JAMA study of six hundred forty-three new drugs finds launch prices kept rising about eight percent a year, and no significant change after the law, though the estimate is uncertain.

On innovation: an industry-funded study reports a sixty-eight percent fall in early investment in pill-type drugs, which drives the push to give pills the same eleven years as biologics. Independent work finds overall trial activity stable.

On costs to others: surveys report pharmacy cash-flow strain, models project lost margins for long-term-care pharmacies, and one economist projects billions in lost revenue for three-forty-B hospitals.

## 1. Verdict on the Headline Claim

**Claim under review:** *"Most of the county-to-county variation and of the strong correlations is reproduced by the model inputs."*

**Verdict: Holds with two essential qualifications that must be stated alongside the claim.**

1. **What holds unconditionally:** Across the 40 age-adjusted PLACES measures, linear regression on county ACS demographic and poverty covariates plus state fixed effects reproduces a median **97.1% of county-to-county variance out-of-sample** (10-fold CV $R^2 = 0.9711$; 5-fold CV $R^2 = 0.9709$; in-sample $R^2 = 0.9735$). Even **without state fixed effects**, the 17 non-collinear county ACS covariates alone reproduce a median **83.3% of variance in-sample** (5-fold CV $R^2 = 0.830$). Across all 780 measure pairs, the median absolute correlation drops from $|r_{\text{raw}}| = 0.597$ to $|r_{\text{partial}}| = 0.160$ after removing county covariates and state fixed effects (and from $0.720$ to $0.280$ among the 494 pairs with $|r_{\text{raw}}| \ge 0.50$). In the uninsurance benchmark restricted to counties with $\ge 20,000$ adults (where ACS sampling noise is only 2.7% of total variance), covariates plus state explain **98.2%** of PLACES `access2_crudeprev` (CV $R^2 = 0.979$) versus **85.7%** of directly measured ACS uninsurance (CV $R^2 = 0.832$–$0.839$, or **88.1%** in-sample / **85.5%** CV after disattenuating for ACS sampling error). Thus, PLACES county estimates contain only $\sim 2\%$–$3\%$ incremental county-specific variance beyond linear combinations of ACS covariates and state intercepts, whereas a comparable directly measured rate retains $\sim 12\%$–$15\%$ genuine local county variation.
2. **Qualification 1 (State fixed effects are not synthetic demographic inputs):** Grouping 50 state fixed effects with county ACS covariates overstates what the demographic/poverty inputs alone reproduce for several measures. In CDC's Multilevel Regression and Poststratification (MRP) framework, state random effects are estimated from thousands of BRFSS respondents per state and reflect real observed state-level prevalence differences (policies, environment, and survey administration). County covariates alone explain $83.3\%$ at the median, while state fixed effects add the remaining $14.0$ percentage points to reach $97.4\%$ (and add $30$–$49$ percentage points for state-driven measures such as `depression`, `casthma`, `highchol`, `binge`, `dental`, `checkup`, and `cholscreen`).
3. **Qualification 2 ("Share of raw covariance remaining" overstates correlation collapse among the strongest pairs):** Because $\text{Cov}(e_1, e_2)/\text{Cov}(y_1, y_2) = (r_{\text{partial}}/r_{\text{raw}})\sqrt{(1-R_1^2)(1-R_2^2)}$, whenever $R_1^2, R_2^2 \approx 0.98$, the variance-shrinkage factor $\sqrt{(1-R_1^2)(1-R_2^2)} \approx 0.02$ forces the residual covariance share to be $\sim 1\%$–$2\%$ even if the residuals remain strongly correlated. Among the 279 pairs with $|r_{\text{raw}}| \ge 0.70$, the median residual covariance share is only **1.4%** (`0.0137`), yet the median absolute partial correlation is still **0.513** (and for `diabetes ~ foodinsecu`, $r$ drops from **0.937** to **0.623** on the linear scale, or **0.480** on the logit scale). For the strongest pairs, model inputs plus state account for nearly all *covariance* (by shrinking the variance of each measure by $\sim 98\%$), and they substantially attenuate the *correlation*, but a moderate-to-strong residual correlation often remains, partly driven by shared logistic nonlinearity and poststratification mismatches.

## 4. Eight Ranked Methodological Findings

### Finding 1 (Rank 1): Including 50 state fixed effects alongside county covariates conflates observed BRFSS state prevalence with synthetic demographic projection, overstating what "model inputs" explain for state-driven measures.
In CDC's MRP specification, $\text{logit}(p_{c,j,s}) = x_c'\beta + z_j\gamma + u_s + v_j$, the state random intercept $\hat{u}_s$ is estimated from thousands of BRFSS interviews per state. Because state sample sizes are large, empirical Bayes shrinkage on $\hat{u}_s$ is negligible; $\hat{u}_s$ reflects **observed state-level survey differences** (state health policies such as Medicaid expansion or cigarette excise taxes, regional dietary/behavioral baselines, and survey administration differences), not synthetic extrapolation from Census demographics. Across the 40 `adjprev` measures, county covariates alone yield a median $R^2$ of **0.833** (mean **0.781**), whereas adding 50 state fixed effects adds **14.0 percentage points** at the median (to **0.974**) and **16.6 percentage points** at the mean (to **0.947**). For measures with weak demographic gradients or strong state policy/reporting effects, state fixed effects account for 30 to 49 percentage points of the claimed explainability: `depression` ($R^2 = 0.468$ covariates-only vs. $0.955$ with state FE), `casthma` ($0.520$ vs. $0.961$), `highchol` ($0.503$ vs. $0.889$), `binge` ($0.546$ vs. $0.941$), `dental` ($0.558$ vs. $0.961$), `checkup` ($0.597$ vs. $0.958$), `bpmed` ($0.648$ vs. $0.973$), and `cholscreen` ($0.648$ vs. $0.984$). Moreover, state fixed effects absorb between-state variation in the county random effects ($\bar{v}_{\cdot s}$), overstating how much county covariates alone explain. Interestingly, for `diabetes ~ foodinsecu` (`adjprev`), controlling *only* for county covariates yields a lower partial correlation ($r = 0.280$) than controlling for county covariates plus state fixed effects ($r = 0.623$), because state intercepts in the two models move in opposite directions relative to their shared poverty gradient and suppress the within-state residual correlation when omitted.

### Finding 2 (Rank 2): "Share of raw covariance remaining in the residuals" ($\text{Cov}(e_1, e_2)/\text{Cov}(y_1, y_2)$) is a misleading summary of correlation survival because it multiplies the correlation ratio by $\sqrt{(1-R_1^2)(1-R_2^2)} \approx 0.02$ and is unbounded.
Because $\text{SD}(e_k) = \text{SD}(y_k)\sqrt{1 - R_k^2}$, the ratio of residual covariance to raw covariance satisfies the exact identity:
$$\frac{\text{Cov}(e_1, e_2)}{\text{Cov}(y_1, y_2)} = \frac{r_{e_1, e_2}}{r_{y_1, y_2}} \sqrt{(1 - R_1^2)(1 - R_2^2)}$$
This metric conflates **univariate variance reduction** ($\sqrt{(1-R_1^2)(1-R_2^2)}$) with **pairwise residual dependence** ($r_{e_1, e_2}$). When $R_1^2 = 0.981$ (`diabetes_adjprev` on the 2,299-county subset) and $R_2^2 = 0.983$ (`foodinsecu_adjprev`), the scale factor $\sqrt{(1-R_1^2)(1-R_2^2)}$ is **0.0182**. Consequently, **even if the residuals of diabetes and food insecurity were 100% correlated ($r_{e_1, e_2} = 1.00$)**, the "share of raw covariance remaining" could not exceed $0.0182 / 0.9366 = \mathbf{1.94\%}$! Reporting that only `0.012` (1.2%) of the raw covariance remains makes it sound as though 98.8% of the correlation vanished, when in fact two-thirds of the correlation survives ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.623$ vs. $r_{\text{raw}} = 0.937$). Across all 279 `adjprev` pairs with $|r_{\text{raw}}| \ge 0.70$, the median `cov_share_remaining` is `0.0137` (1.4%), yet the median $|r_{\text{partial}}|$ is **0.513** (and 65 of those 279 pairs retain $|r_{\text{partial}}| \ge 0.70$). In addition, unlike variance components, $\text{Cov}(\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_2)$ and $\text{Cov}(e_1, e_2)$ can take opposite signs: 57 of 780 `adjprev` pairs (and 128 of 780 `crudeprev` pairs) have negative `cov_share_remaining`, and pairs with small raw correlations produce arbitrary ratios (e.g., `depression ~ loneliness` has $r_{\text{raw}} = 0.050$, $r_{\text{partial}} = 0.293$, and `cov_share_remaining = 0.426`).

### Finding 3 (Rank 3): What the OLS residual contains in an MRP model, and how linear regression understates input explainability while inflating residual correlations via shared logistic nonlinearity.
In CDC's MRP pipeline, county $j$'s published prevalence is $\hat{Y}_j = \sum_{c=1}^{C} w_{c,j} \sigma(x_c'\hat{\beta} + z_j\hat{\gamma} + \hat{u}_{s(j)} + \hat{v}_j)$, where $\sigma(\eta) = (1 + e^{-\eta})^{-1}$ and $w_{c,j}$ are poststratification cell weights ($C = 13 \times 2 \times 8 \times 4 = 832$ age-sex-race-education cells). A second-order expansion around the county mean predictor shows that the linear OLS residual $e_j = \hat{Y}_j - \hat{\theta}_0 - \bar{x}_j'\hat{\theta}_1 - \hat{\alpha}_{s(j)}$ contains four distinct terms:
1. **Rescaled county random effect:** $\approx \bar{p}_j(1-\bar{p}_j)\hat{v}_j$, where $\hat{v}_j$ is the empirical Bayes BLUP of the county random intercept from BRFSS.
2. **Logistic link curvature and baseline-slope interactions:** Under a logit link, $\partial p / \partial \eta = p(1-p)$ and $\partial^2 p / \partial \eta^2 = p(1-p)(1-2p)$. A county in a high-prevalence state or with high poverty has a different effective linear slope on race, education, and age than a county near $p = 0.05$, and extreme counties bend along the sigmoid.
3. **Poststratification cell-interaction effects (within-county ecological covariance):** Because $\sigma(\cdot)$ is applied to cross-classified cells $w_{c,j}$ (and even on a linear scale if age-adjustment weights interact with sex/race/education across age strata), within-county correlations among age, race, sex, and education (e.g., an older White population alongside a younger Hispanic population) enter $\hat{Y}_j$ but are omitted by 1D county marginal shares $\bar{x}_j$.
4. **Covariate and population-vintage mismatch ($\Delta X_j \hat{\beta}$):** Differences between the analyst's ACS proxy covariates and the exact poststratification inputs used by CDC.

**Empirical verification:** Because terms (2), (3), and (4) are deterministic functions of the MRP inputs rather than county random effects, linear OLS **understates** how much the MRP inputs explain and **overstates** the residual correlation between pairs of measures that share the same curvature. Fitting the exact same covariates plus state FEs on the **logit scale** ($\text{logit}(Y_j/100)$) increases the median 5-fold CV $R^2$ across the 40 `adjprev` measures from **0.9709 to 0.9755** (reducing median unexplained variance by 16%, from 2.91% to 2.45%) and cuts the `diabetes ~ foodinsecu` (`adjprev`) residual correlation from **0.6235 to 0.4800** (**0.4765** when quadratic and poverty-interaction terms are added). Thus, nearly a quarter of the apparent `0.623` residual correlation between diabetes and food insecurity is shared logistic nonlinearity rather than correlated county random effects.

### Finding 4 (Rank 4): Four covariate mismatches between `acs_county_2023.csv` and CDC's MRP specification explain the lowest-$R^2$ outliers (`mammouse`, `teethlost`, `colon_screen`) and leak shared input error into the residuals.
Checking the CDC PLACES methodology page (`cdc.gov/places/methodology/index.html`), Greenlund et al. (2022), and Wang et al. (2018) against `acs_county_2023.csv` identifies four specific covariate mismatches:
1. **Target age/sex subpopulation mismatch:** Four PLACES measures apply to restricted age/sex universes: `mammouse` (women aged 50–74), `teethlost` (adults aged $\ge 65$), `colon_screen` (adults aged 45–75), and `access2` (adults aged 18–64). Regressing these subpopulation outcomes on whole-county covariates (`all-age` race, `18+` sex and poverty, `25+` education) introduces subpopulation composition mismatch, which directly accounts for why `mammouse` (CV $R^2 = 0.727$), `teethlost` ($0.744$), and `colon_screen` ($0.833$) sit at the very bottom of the $R^2$ distribution.
2. **Universe mismatch in race/ethnicity (`B03002`) and education (`B15003`):** `acs_county_2023.csv` computes race/ethnicity shares from Table `B03002` (**total population including children 0–17**) and education shares from Table `B15003` (**adults 25+**, excluding 18–24), whereas PLACES models **adults 18+**. In counties where racial/ethnic minorities are substantially younger than non-Hispanic White residents, or in college counties with large 18–24 populations, these proxies diverge from the 18+ poststratification frame.
3. **Coarsened age categories (7 vs. 13 bands):** PLACES MRP uses 13 adult age bands (18–24, five-year bands 25–29 through 75–79, and $\ge 80$), whereas `acs_county_2023.csv` collapses age into 7 ten-year bands ending at $\ge 75$.
4. **Population vintage and BRFSS cycle differences:** PLACES 2025 poststratifies to **Census Vintage 2023** county population estimates (and uses BRFSS 2022 for 5 biennial measures and BRFSS 2023 for 35 measures), whereas `acs_county_2023.csv` uses 2019–2023 5-year ACS averages. Because any two PLACES measures share the exact same Vintage 2023 poststratification population counts, their shared discrepancy $\Delta X_j = X_j^{\text{Vintage2023}} - X_j^{\text{ACS5yr}}$ enters both OLS residuals and positively biases their residual correlation.

### Finding 5 (Rank 5): ACS sampling error accounts for 10.7% of all-county variance in the uninsurance benchmark (making the all-county gap unfair), but only 2.7% in counties with $\ge 20,000$ adults (confirming a genuine ~10–12 pp MRP smoothing gap).
Using the published 90% margins of error (`B27010_M018`, `M033`, `M034`, `M050`) from `acsdt5y2023-b27010.dat` ($\text{SE} = \text{MOE}/1.645$), we quantified the exact sampling error variance of `acs_uninsured_19_64`:
- **All common counties ($N = 2,956$):** Cross-county variance is $52.07\text{ pp}^2$, and mean within-county sampling error variance $\overline{\text{SE}^2}$ is $5.58\text{ pp}^2$ (**10.72% of total variance**; among the 1,471 counties with $<20,000$ adults, sampling noise is **16.15%** of total variance, with median $\text{SE} = 2.02\text{ pp}$). Because independent outcome sampling noise cannot be predicted out-of-sample by true covariates, the maximum achievable $R^2$ (reliability ceiling) across all 2,956 counties is $1 - 0.1072 = \mathbf{0.8928}$. Dividing the observed ACS $R^2$ (`0.7402` in-sample, `0.7176` 5-fold CV) by this ceiling yields a **disattenuated $R^2$ of 0.8291 in-sample (0.8038 CV)**. Thus, more than two-thirds of the jump in ACS $R^2$ when restricting from all counties (`0.740`) to $\ge 20,000$ adults (`0.857`) comes simply from removing small-county ACS sampling noise ($8.0\text{ pp}$ of the $11.7\text{ pp}$ gain).
- **Counties with $\ge 20,000$ adults ($N = 1,485$):** Mean sampling error variance falls by more than fivefold to $1.01\text{ pp}^2$ out of $37.62\text{ pp}^2$ (**2.67% noise share**, median $\text{SE} = 0.81\text{ pp}$, reliability ceiling $\mathbf{0.9733}$). Disattenuating raises the $\ge 20,000$-adult ACS $R^2$ from **0.8574 to 0.8809 in-sample** (and CV $R^2$ from **0.832–0.839 to 0.855–0.862**), compared to PLACES `access2_crudeprev` at **0.9817 in-sample (0.9794 CV)**.
- **Two benchmark nuances:** (1) `acs_uninsured_19_64` covers ages **19–64** (and PLACES `access2_crudeprev` covers **18–64**), whereas the regression covariates include senior age shares (`age_65_74`, `age_75p`) and 18+/25+/all-age demographics; (2) in small counties, `B27010` and the ACS covariate tables come from the same 5-year ACS household sample (which creates a small positive correlation between outcome and covariate sampling errors, while covariate sampling error attenuates $\hat{\beta}$). Even after full disattenuation in $\ge 20,000$-adult counties, a **10.1 pp in-sample (12.4 pp CV) gap** remains between PLACES (`0.982`) and ACS (`0.881` disattenuated), Measuring the genuine sub-state local variation in uninsurance that PLACES's MRP model smooths out.

### Finding 6 (Rank 6): The county random effect $\hat{v}_j$ in PLACES is heavily shrunk and estimated univariately, so residual correlations do not estimate true partial correlations among health outcomes.
Even if the county random effect $\hat{v}_{j,m}$ could be isolated cleanly from nonlinearity and covariate mismatch, CDC fits a **separate univariate** multilevel model for each measure $m$. The empirical Bayes prediction for county $j$ is $\hat{v}_{j,m} \approx \lambda_{j,m} \bar{r}_{j,m}$, where $\lambda_{j,m} = \sigma_{v,m}^2 / (\sigma_{v,m}^2 + \sigma_{e,m}^2 / n_j)$ depends on the county's BRFSS sample size $n_j$. In small and medium counties where $n_j$ is small, $\lambda_{j,m}$ shrinks $\hat{v}_{j,m}$ strongly toward zero (which is why $\hat{v}_{j,m}$ accounts for $<2\%$ of variance in $\hat{Y}_{j,m}$). Across counties, the variance and covariance of $\hat{v}_{j,m}$ are dominated by large urban counties where $n_j$ (and thus $\lambda_{j,m}$) is large. Moreover, for two measures collected in the same BRFSS wave (e.g., 2023 BRFSS `diabetes` and `foodinsecu`), $\bar{r}_{j,1}$ and $\bar{r}_{j,2}$ come from the **same individual BRFSS respondents** in county $j$, so $\text{Corr}(\hat{v}_{j,1}, \hat{v}_{j,2})$ mixes true county-level residual comorbidity with individual-level sampling error covariance within the county's BRFSS sample.

### Finding 7 (Rank 7): Kong & Zhang (2020) and external validations show that high input $R^2$ proves *informational redundancy* of PLACES in ecological regressions, not that the raw epidemiological correlations are fake.
Kong & Zhang (2020, *AJPH*) warn that PLACES/500 Cities estimates are "akin to synthetic estimates" whose cross-area associations with demographic inputs or with one another reflect the group compositional variables built into the model, and that secondary regressions rarely account for estimation uncertainty (citing Lewis & Linzer 2005). However, our ACS benchmark proves that in reality, county demographics, poverty, and state fixed effects genuinely explain **85.7% (88.1% noise-adjusted)** of true county variation in uninsurance among counties with $\ge 20,000$ adults. Likewise, county demographic and poverty gradients in diabetes and food insecurity are real epidemiological phenomena, not statistical inventions. The covariate test therefore does **not** show that diabetes and food insecurity are unrelated in reality; rather, it proves that **PLACES county estimates add almost no county-level observational signal ($\le 2\%$–$3\%$ variance) beyond ACS covariates and state intercepts**, making ecological regressions of PLACES measures on area socioeconomic indices (such as the Social Vulnerability Index) circular.

### Finding 8 (Rank 8): Non-random state missingness across PLACES measures (3,143 vs. 2,956 vs. 2,299 counties) affects separate-vs-pairwise residual orthogonality and benchmark sample coverage.
PLACES 2025 has three nested county coverage tiers: 5 measures from 2022 BRFSS cover all **3,143** counties; 28 measures from 2023 BRFSS cover **2,956** counties (omitting all 120 counties in Kentucky and 67 in Pennsylvania); and 7 optional-module SDOH measures (`foodinsecu`, `foodstamp`, `housinsecu`, `shututility`, `lacktrpt`, `loneliness`, `emotionspt`) cover **2,299** counties across 39 states. If each measure is regressed on its own available sample first ($N=2,956$ for `diabetes`, $N=2,299$ for `foodinsecu`) and residuals are correlated on the $N=2,299$ intersection, the `diabetes` residual is no longer orthogonal to the covariates on the 39-state subset, yielding $r_{\text{partial}} = 0.6059$ instead of $0.6235$ when both are refitted on the common $N=2,299$ sample (though `cov_share_remaining = 0.0121` is algebraically invariant because `foodinsecu`'s residual is orthogonal to the 39-state design matrix). Similarly, in the uninsurance benchmark, filtering ACS uninsurance to counties with non-missing PLACES `access2` drops all 187 counties in KY and PA ($N=2,956$ vs. $3,143$ all counties; $N=1,485$ vs. $1,589$ for $\ge 20,000$ adults), even though ACS uninsurance and covariates are fully observed there ($R^2 = 0.7382$ on $N=3,143$; $R^2 = 0.8544$ on $N=1,589$).

US PUBLIC DATA STUDY · UPDATED 2026-10-20

Medicare's first negotiated drug prices: what moved in 2026, and what we expect in 2027

On 2026-01-01 the first ten prices negotiated under the Inflation Reduction Act took effect in Medicare Part D. Free federal data show what changed: plans now price these drugs at the negotiated price, gross spending per prescription fell by about two thirds, several manufacturers cut list prices on the same day, and pharmacy invoice costs stay well above the new price for most of them.

BACKGROUND

How Medicare drug pricing works

A brand-name drug passes through several hands before it reaches a Medicare patient, and money flows back along a different route. There is no single "price": the list price (WAC) is published by the manufacturer; pharmacies buy at close to list; plans pay pharmacies a contracted amount at the counter; and manufacturers later pay plans confidential rebates in exchange for good formulary placement. In 2024, rebates cut the gross Part D cost of the first ten negotiated drugs by nearly 45% (MedPAC, March 2026).

Player	What it does
Manufacturer	Makes the drug, publishes its list price (WAC), pays rebates and discounts afterwards.
Wholesaler	Buys from the manufacturer, sells to pharmacies.
Pharmacy	Buys the drug (NADAC measures its invoice cost), dispenses it, is paid by the plan and the patient.
Part D plan and its PBM	Private plans that Medicare pays to cover outpatient drugs. The pharmacy benefit manager builds the formulary, negotiates rebates and sets pharmacy payment.
Medicare	Pays most Part D costs through the plans; since 2026 also negotiates prices for selected drugs.
Patient	Pays premium, deductible and copay or coinsurance, up to an annual cap ($2,000 in 2025, $2,100 in 2026).
The negotiation program

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 lets Medicare negotiate a Maximum Fair Price (MFP) for high-spending drugs without generic or biosimilar competition: small molecules at least 7 years past approval and biologics 11 years past licensure when selected. For the rounds taking effect in 2026 to 2028 the law sets a ceiling: at least 25% off the average non-federal price for most drugs, at least 60% for drugs 16 or more years past approval, or the Part D net price if lower. At the pharmacy, plan payment plus patient cost sharing cannot exceed the MFP plus dispensing fees. Pharmacies that bought at list are made whole by manufacturer refunds, by default list price minus MFP, exchanged through CMS's Medicare Transaction Facilitator; CMS reports refunds arriving within 21 days of the date of service on average.

A drug leaves once CMS finds a generic or biosimilar is really marketed: Entresto, Stelara, Xarelto and NovoLog/Fiasp leave on 2027-01-01. The Supreme Court declined to hear the manufacturers' challenges on 2026-05-18.

The design is a before/after comparison against drugs not yet affected: the 15 drugs negotiated for 2027 (no MFP in 2026), the 15 selected for 2028, and about 740 other brand-name products. This describes what happened; it does not by itself separate the MFP from list-price strategy, rebate changes, generic entry or the 2025 Part D redesign.

FINDING 1

Gross Part D spending per claim fell at least 30% for every negotiated drug

Between 2025 and 2026 Q1 the median change across the ten drugs was −64%, from −30% for the NovoLog entry to −71% for Enbrel. Holding each group's 2025 drug mix fixed, the ten fell −61%, while drugs negotiated for 2027 changed +1.5%, drugs selected for 2028 +1.2% and other brand-name products +1.1%. The NovoLog entry pools two brands: NovoLog itself (list price already cut in 2024) fell 9%, Fiasp 72%.

This is what the payment rule would produce: the law caps the Part D price of a selected drug at the MFP plus dispensing fees, and the spending file is built from claim amounts. It is consistent with MFP-based payment; it does not prove it for every claim.

FINDING 2

On the day the MFPs began, four of the ten cut their list prices, and so did related brands

NADAC records WAC-based cuts effective 2026-01-01 of 43% for Eliquis, 44% for Jardiance, 37% for Farxiga and 75% for Fiasp. Januvia, and Janumet in the 2027 group, had been cut 42% on 2025-01-01, and Imbruvica (no NADAC) on 2025-12-22 according to NCPA's notice: six of the ten have had list-price cuts since 2024. Most of the other-brand cuts are not independent: 30 of the 33 in January 2026 are Boehringer Ingelheim's empagliflozin combinations, cut by the same 44.4% on the same day as Jardiance.

Why cut list prices? Candidate explanations, not established: under the default refund (list minus MFP) a lower list price shrinks the refunds owed; Bristol Myers Squibb told Reuters a lower Eliquis list price helps avoid Medicare inflation penalties; the cuts also removed the inflation part of the 340B discount, ending "penny pricing" from 2026-07-01 (Eliquis $245 a package, Pennsylvania Office of Rural Health); and for insulin the Medicaid rebate cap ended on 2024-01-01. Their net effect on manufacturer revenue is not measured.

FINDING 3

Pharmacy invoice cost stays well above the MFP for most of these drugs

NADAC, the average invoice cost of retail community pharmacies, was 2.2 to 4.0 times the 2026 MFP per unit at the end of 2025 (NovoLog was already at 1.0). After the January cuts it fell to 0.99 for Fiasp, 1.33 for Eliquis, 1.65 for Jardiance and 2.00 for Farxiga. Januvia (2.70), Xarelto (2.84), Entresto (2.22), Stelara (3.32) and Enbrel (3.57) stayed high, some rising with 2026 list-price increases.

FINDING 4

Reported plan prices sit at the MFP from January 2026

CMS's quarterly plan pricing files list each plan's reported unit price for the drugs it covers, in the same billing units as the MFP. For the ten drugs, plan prices were a median 2.77 times the MFP in plan year 2025. In the January 2026 release the median was 1.000: 92.7% of plan-product prices were within 0.5% of the MFP (99.7% weighting drugs by their claims), 4.9% below it (the MFP is a ceiling) and 2.3% above, all of those on unbranded ustekinumab products grouped with Stelara. By October 2026, none were above. The fifteen drugs negotiated for 2027 stayed above their future MFP.

CONTEXT

What others have found

A scoping scan of about 180 papers and grey-literature items (2026-10-20) found no published measure of realized 2026 spending across the ten drugs, and no weekly product-level NADAC-to-MFP series through 2026. The closest work covers Stelara alone (Castle et al. 2026, JAMA Network Open) or compares price benchmarks at one point in time (PORTAL, 2024). The list-price cuts are documented by NCPA, 46brooklyn and Drug Channels but not yet in academic work.

Question	What the evidence says
How much does it save?	CMS: 22% (about $6 billion) against 2023 net spending for round one; 44% (about $12 billion, or 36% counting coverage-gap discounts) for round two against 2024. Jeon et al. 2026 project 2026 net spending on the ten drugs at $23.8 billion vs $26.1 billion without negotiation (−8.8%). Wouters et al. 2024 argue CMS's 22% overstates savings.
How deep are the discounts?	Round-one MFPs were 8% to 42% below estimated net prices (Wouters et al. 2024). Round-two MFPs were a median 38% below estimated net prices (range 15% to 60%) but a median 108% above purchasing-power-adjusted prices in 19 reference countries (Gabriel et al. 2026).
Do patients save?	Modeled average annual out-of-pocket savings across six drugs of $685 in stand-alone plans and $470 in Medicare Advantage; Stelara, Imbruvica and Enbrel users reach the $2,100 cap either way (Castle et al. 2026, Annals). Projections, not observed savings.
Access and plan behavior	Every plan covers all ten drugs in 2026; step therapy on negotiated diabetes drugs rose to 5.0% (Jain and McCoy 2026). Access to non-selected brands in competitive classes fell 11.8 points in stand-alone plans, 2024 to 2026 (Zheng et al. 2026).
Launch prices	Among 643 new drugs approved 2008 to 2025, launch prices rose about 7.8% a year before the law, with no significant change in trend afterwards; the estimate is imprecise (Shanmugam et al. 2026).
Innovation	Industry-funded work reports a 68% peak-to-2024 fall in early-stage small-molecule investment in one cohort (Schulthess et al. 2025, descriptive); Kang et al. 2026 find overall trial activity stable. Unsettled.
Pharmacies and 340B	Survey-reported refund delays (NCPA); projected margin losses for long-term-care pharmacies above 85% (Bhardwaj et al. 2026) and lower 340B net revenue (Courtemanche 2026). Surveys and projections, not measured losses.

LOOKING AHEAD

What we expect in 2027, written down before the data arrive

The 2026 results were seen before any test was designed, so they cannot confirm a hypothesis. The fifteen drugs whose MFPs start on 2027-01-01 can. On 2026-10-20 we registered five predictions in git (commit b24d4c8, before opening any 2027 file); clarifications added after review are dated amendments that never change a registered number.

The rule. If the MFP binds, a drug's gross spending per claim should fall by about 1/r − 1, where r is the plan price divided by the coming MFP in the last plan pricing file before it starts. It has no fitted parameter. Applied after the fact to the 2026 drugs, it missed by at most 2.1 points for eight of the ten; the two misses have known causes (Entresto lost most claims to generics; the NovoLog entry pools two differently priced brands). Across all ten the mean absolute error is 4.7 points, and at fixed claim mix it predicted −62.2% against −61.4% measured.

Amendments after review

Amendment 1 (2026-10-20, after the Codex review). Codex reproduced all 221 baseline numbers. The amendment fixes how each prediction is scored without changing any registered number: every clause is scored separately as pass, fail or not evaluable, with no overall verdict; the unit is plan x segment x product rows with a 30-day supply against the MFP file as downloaded on 2026-10-20; missing products are listed, never counted as passes. The semaglutide escape clause is withdrawn, so a semaglutide median below the MFP is a fail of P1 (Medicare's $50-copay GLP-1 Bridge runs outside Part D through 2027). It also corrects the registration's data note: the monthly 2027 release CMS posted on 2026-10-08 has no pricing table, so the plan-price test waits for the January 2027 quarterly release. The calibration is reported in full: across all ten drugs the mean absolute error is 4.7 points and the rank correlation 0.72, so P2's rank clause (0.8) is a harder test than the rule has passed so far.

Amendment 2 (2026-10-20, after the Agy review). Each Agy finding was checked against the data before use. Applied: a code fix (the Fiasp row was dropped from one baseline table) and five pre-specified sensitivities: a 2026 Q4 baseline (Xifaxan's plan price rose 16% during 2026, so a full-year baseline will understate its fall), semaglutide by brand (Ozempic r = 3.66, Wegovy r = 3.33), brand-only plan prices, a 2026 Q1 baseline for the drugs leaving and continuing, and a 2028 comparator without drugs that cut list prices (Trulicity is 42% of its claims). Not applied: Agy listed wrong values for Gabriel et al.'s discounts while calling them an exact match, described a scoring band that does not exist, and summarized a paper with figures not in its text. The rank-correlation threshold stays at 0.8.

LIMITS

How to read this
Gross, not net. Part D spending is before manufacturer rebates; drug-level net spending is not public. Nothing here estimates net savings.
Preliminary quarter. 2026 Q1 Part D data were extracted three months after the quarter and can be revised.
Mix, not only price. Spending per claim moves with days supplied, strength, form and substitution. Entresto (brand claims down 87% annualized after generic entry), Stelara (down 44% after biosimilars) and Farxiga may reflect mix as well as price.
Name-based groups. Part D rows carry brand names, not NDCs; "other brands" is a name-based classification that still contains a few generic products.
NADAC is retail, per unit, unweighted. It covers retail community pharmacies; medians across brand NDCs are not weighted by claims.
Plan prices are schedules. SPUF lists one proxy product per drug concept and reports schedule prices, not realized claim payments.
Not causal. The design does not separate the MFP from list-price strategy, the 2024 Medicaid rebate-cap change, generic and biosimilar entry, the 2025 Part D redesign or anticipation by later cohorts.

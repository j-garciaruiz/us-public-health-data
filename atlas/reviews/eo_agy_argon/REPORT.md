## 1. Verdict on the Headline Question

**Question:** *Does the CDC PLACES county estimate of a condition carry information about a directly measured county outcome beyond what county demographics plus state already predict? Is "PLACES adds little beyond demographics for predicting measured outcomes" a fair conclusion?*

**Verdict: Yes on predictive variance ($\Delta R^2_{\text{CV}}$ is under 1 percentage point for 37 of the 42 outcome-sample pairs and never exceeds 2.4 percentage points), but the conclusion requires three substantive epidemiological qualifications so that construct mismatch and measurement noise are not confused with model error.**

1. **What holds unconditionally (informational redundancy of PLACES once demographics and state are in the model):**
   Across all 21 outcome pairs (11 CMS Mapping Medicare Disparities [MMD] claims conditions, 6 ACS 5-year disability measures, and 4 CDC WONDER 7-year pooled cause-specific mortality SMRs), adding the matching CDC PLACES county estimate to a linear regression on county ACS demographics plus state fixed effects (plus Medicare Advantage penetration for Medicare claims) yields a median out-of-sample 10-fold CV $R^2$ gain of:
   - **Medicare claims (11 pairs):** **+0.54 percentage points** (`+0.0054`, range `-0.0002` to `+0.0073`) across all shared counties ($N = 2,898\text{–}2,918$), and **+0.70 percentage points** (`+0.0070`, range `-0.00005` to `+0.0242`) in larger counties with $\ge 5,000$ Original Medicare beneficiaries ($N = 1,016\text{–}1,017$).
   - **ACS disability (6 pairs):** **-0.02 percentage points** (`-0.0002`, range `-0.0008` to `+0.0014` using `adjprev`; `-0.0005` using `crudeprev`) across all shared counties ($N = 2,956$), and **+0.10 percentage points** (`+0.0010` using `adjprev`; `+0.0023` using `crudeprev`, max `+0.0086` for mobility) in larger counties with $\ge 20,000$ adults ($N = 1,485$).
   - **WONDER mortality SMRs (4 pairs):** **+0.38 percentage points** (`+0.0038`, range `+0.0006` to `+0.0090`) across all unsuppressed shared counties ($N = 2,722\text{–}2,931$), and **+0.04 percentage points** (`+0.0004`, range `-0.0002` to `+0.0107` for COPD/CLRD) in larger counties with $\ge 100$ expected deaths ($N = 990\text{–}2,566$).
   For an "Expected vs. Observed" county atlas where "Expected" is already predicted from county demographics and state, adding PLACES as an extra predictor changes the expected baseline by almost nothing because county demographics and state already explain **95% to 99%** of the variance in 19 of the 21 PLACES measures (`79.6%–81.5%` for `obesity_adjprev` and `79.3%–89.0%` for `highchol_adjprev`), leaving only $1\%\text{–}5\%$ unique county residual variance in PLACES (`r_partial` median `0.104` Medicare all, `0.128` Medicare large; `0.023` disability all, `0.084` disability large; `0.088` mortality all, `0.074` mortality large).
2. **Qualification 1 (Why raw correlations with Medicare and mortality are modest or near zero for some conditions):**
   When PLACES correlates weakly with Medicare claims (e.g., `asthma` $r_{\text{raw}} = 0.061$ all / `-0.023` large; `cancer_4_sites` $r_{\text{raw}} = 0.085$ all / `0.050` large; `depression` $r_{\text{raw}} = 0.285$ all) or leaves $35\%\text{–}60\%$ of variance in claims and mortality unexplained even in large counties, that gap is **not** simply "local prevalence that PLACES missed." Rather, the benchmarks measure **different populations and different epidemiological constructs**:
   - Medicare MMD covers **Original Medicare (Fee-For-Service) beneficiaries** (mostly aged $\ge 65$ plus disabled $<65$), whereas PLACES models **all resident adults aged $\ge 18$**.
   - Claims prevalence reflects **utilization, diagnostic intensity, coding practices, and survival** (e.g., MMD `cancer_4_sites` measures active 2023 claims treatment for breast, colorectal, lung, or prostate cancer in FFS Medicare, whereas PLACES `cancer` measures lifetime self-reported history of *any* non-skin cancer among adults 18+).
   - Mortality SMRs reflect **incidence $\times$ case fatality** (acute/chronic care quality, emergency transport, and county coroner/physician death-certificate coding habits, especially for diabetes), not disease prevalence alone.
3. **Qualification 2 (Small-area sampling noise in SMRs, claims, and ACS):**
   In small counties, Poisson death-count noise accounts for **5.5% to 18.5%** of total cross-county SMR variance (`18.5%` for stroke, `9.8%` for diabetes and COPD, `5.5%` for heart disease), and CMS integer rounding plus small FFS denominators add noise to MMD claims. An unshrunk "Observed minus Expected" map across all 3,143 counties will predominantly highlight **tiny rural counties with extreme random sampling fluctuations** unless empirical Bayes shrinkage and reliability flags are applied.
4. **Qualification 3 (Bugs identified in the current pipeline must be fixed before building the atlas):**
   Our audit in Section 4 uncovered five concrete bugs in [`scripts/build_mortality_smr.py`](file:///Users/hari/Documents/VSC%20Python/US%20Data/scripts/build_mortality_smr.py) and [`scripts/expected_vs_observed.py`](file:///Users/hari/Documents/VSC%20Python/US%20Data/scripts/expected_vs_observed.py): (1) **28 county-cause cells with 0 deaths (`0 <= 9`)** are published as `smr = 0.0` (`suppressed = False`) in `wonder_county_smr.csv` and included in `places_vs_observed.csv` and `observed_minus_expected_by_county.csv`, violating the NCHS/docstring rule that counts of 9 or fewer get no SMR and creating non-monotone `{0} U [10, inf)` left-truncation; (2) Kusilvak Census Area, AK (`02270` $\to$ `02158`, a 1-to-1 2015 rename identical to Oglala Lakota `46113` $\to$ `46102`) is misclassified as a county split and dropped; (3) the saved atlas file [`observed_minus_expected_by_county.csv`](file:///Users/hari/Documents/VSC%20Python/US%20Data/outputs/expected_vs_observed/observed_minus_expected_by_county.csv) **drops all 187 counties in Kentucky and Pennsylvania** across all 21 outcomes because it filters on non-missing PLACES before fitting the demographic-only expected model; (4) `observed_minus_expected_by_county.csv` saves **out-of-fold 10-fold CV predictions from a single random split** (`seed=20261020`) rather than full-sample or empirical-Bayes predictions, giving District of Columbia (`11001`) a national-average state intercept when held out; and (5) `cv_predict` has a latent collinearity bug when the dropped reference state (`AK`) is unseen in a training fold.

### 2.1 Aligning Age Bands and Defining County Person-Years

1. **Age-band alignment (`0–4` through `85+`):**
   CDC WONDER reports national 2018–2024 deaths and pooled person-years across 11 age groups (`< 1 year`, `1–4 years`, and nine 10-year bands `5–14` through `75–84` plus `85+`), plus `Not Stated` (`NS`). In ACS Table `B01001` (Sex by Age), the youngest category is `Under 5 years` (`B01001_E003` Male + `B01001_E027` Female), while the remaining 22 sex-specific age cells aggregate exactly into the nine 10-year WONDER bands (`5–14` through `85+`). Verify check: across all 3,222 county rows in `acsdt5y2023-b01001.dat`, the sum of the 10 aligned age bands equals total population `B01001_E001` with maximum absolute difference `0.0`.
   Pooling WONDER's `< 1 year` and `1–4 years` into `0–4 years` ($P_{0\text{–}4}^{\text{nat}} = 25,878,128 + 107,286,287 = 133,164,415$ person-years) yields exact national 2018–2024 age-specific mortality rates $R_{a,c}^{\text{nat}} = D_{a,c}^{\text{nat}} / P_a^{\text{nat}}$ across the 10 bands. (In `national_age_2018_2024.txt`, `< 1 year` diabetes deaths are `"Suppressed"`, i.e., $1\text{–}9$ deaths over 7 years; setting that cell to $0$ vs. midpoint $5$ changes national expected diabetes deaths by $5$ out of $662,820$, or $0.00075\%$.)
2. **Two constructions for county person-years by age ($N_{i,a}$):**
   Because WONDER pools 7 calendar years (2018–2024) while ACS `B01001` is a 5-year (2019–2023) annual population estimate, there are two ways to construct county person-years in age band $a$:
   - **Construction A (`acs7`, direct ACS person-years):** $N_{i,a}^{(\text{ACS}\times 7)} = 7 \times P_{i,a}^{\text{ACS}}$, so $E_i^{(\text{ACS}\times 7)} = \sum_a 7 P_{i,a}^{\text{ACS}} R_{a,c}^{\text{nat}}$.
   - **Construction B (`wpop`, WONDER county person-years $\times$ ACS age shares):** $N_{i,a}^{(\text{wpop})} = P_i^{\text{WONDER}} \times (P_{i,a}^{\text{ACS}} / P_{i,\text{total}}^{\text{ACS}})$, where $P_i^{\text{WONDER}}$ is the 2018–2024 pooled county `Population` reported in `county_cause_2018_2024.txt`.
   We computed and saved **both** constructions in [`recomputed_expected_vs_observed.py`](file:///Users/hari/Documents/VSC%20Python/US%20Data/reviews/eo_agy_argon/recompute_expected_vs_observed.py). Construction B (`wpop`) is preferable for counties with non-missing WONDER population because it keeps the denominator of $E_i$ on the exact same 2018–2024 total person-year base as WONDER's death counts $O_i$, avoiding distortion when a county's population grew rapidly in 2023–2024 or when ACS 5-year total population differs from postcensal estimates.

### 2.3 Audit of County FIPS Code Changes Between WONDER, ACS, and PLACES

Comparing the 3,142 counties in WONDER `county_cause_2018_2024.txt`, the 3,144 counties (50 states + DC) in ACS `B01001`, and the 3,143 counties in `places_county_2025.csv` reveals five exact geographic discrepancies:

1. **Oglala Lakota County, SD (`46113` in WONDER $\to$ `46102` in ACS and PLACES):** Renamed from Shannon County (`46113`) in May 2015 with identical boundaries. Must be remapped 1-to-1 (`46113` $\to$ `46102`).
2. **Kusilvak Census Area, AK (`02270` in WONDER $\to$ `02158` in ACS and PLACES):** Renamed from Wade Hampton Census Area (`02270`) in July 2015 with identical boundaries. Must be remapped 1-to-1 (`02270` $\to$ `02158`). As documented in Section 4 (Bug 2), `scripts/build_mortality_smr.py` failed to remap `02270` $\to$ `02158`, mistakenly claiming in a comment that both Alaska codes split.
3. **Valdez-Cordova Census Area, AK (`02261` in WONDER $\to$ split into `02063` Chugach and `02066` Copper River in ACS and PLACES):** Split on January 2, 2019. WONDER reports pooled 2018–2024 deaths only for the combined area `02261`, whereas ACS 2019–2023 and PLACES 2025 report `02063` and `02066` separately. Because observed deaths in `02261` cannot be split without external assumptions, leaving `02261` unmatched against PLACES is appropriate.
4. **Connecticut's 8 Legacy Counties (`09001`–`09015` in WONDER) vs. 9 Planning Regions (`09110`–`09190` in ACS and PLACES):** Starting with Vintage 2022 and the 2019–2023 ACS, the Census Bureau replaced Connecticut's 8 legacy counties with 9 county-equivalent planning regions whose boundaries cross the old counties. In WONDER `county_cause_2018_2024.txt`, deaths are still tabulated by the 8 legacy counties (`09001`–`09015`), and WONDER reports their `Population` as `"Not Available"` (Caveat 10). Under `acs7` ($7 \times \text{ACS}$), expected deaths can be computed for the 9 planning regions (`09110`–`09190`), and town-level population crosswalks can map legacy counties to planning regions approximately, but without a crosswalk Connecticut cannot be joined directly between WONDER (`09001`–`09015`) and ACS/PLACES (`09110`–`09190`).
5. **Loving County, TX (`48301`):** Present in WONDER (`48301`, where all 4 causes are suppressed) and in ACS (`48301`, population 51), but omitted from PLACES 2025 (`3,143` counties).

### 2.4 Is Indirect Age Standardization Appropriate Here?

- **Why it is necessary and practically sound here:** Direct age standardization at the county level requires county-by-age-by-cause death counts. In public CDC WONDER tables, any county $\times$ 10-year age group $\times$ cause cell with 1–9 deaths is suppressed, which censors the majority of age strata in small and medium counties. Moreover, direct standardization in small counties has high variance because noisy county age-specific rates are multiplied by large standard-population weights. Indirect standardization ($\text{SMR}_i = O_i / E_i$) requires only the county's marginal total deaths $O_i$ and applies precise national age-specific rates $R_{a,c}^{\text{nat}}$ to the county's age structure, minimizing sampling variance.
- **Theoretical caveat (non-comparability across counties when proportionality fails):** Formally, $\text{SMR}_i = \sum_a w_{i,a} (r_{i,a} / R_a^{\text{nat}})$ weights each county's age-specific relative mortality ratios by *that county's own expected deaths* $w_{i,a} = N_{i,a} R_a^{\text{nat}} / E_i$ rather than a common standard population. If relative mortality risk varies by age (for example, if high-poverty rural counties have a relative risk of $2.5$ at ages 45–64 but $1.2$ at ages $85+$), an older county and a younger county with identical age-specific rates will have different SMRs. However, in the Task 2 regressions (`SMR ~ demographics + state + PLACES`), the county's adult age shares (`age_18_24` through `age_75p`) are included directly as covariates, which absorbs first-order residual age-weighting differences across counties.

## 5. Task 4: Eight Ranked Methodological Findings and Atlas Recommendations

### Finding 1 (Rank 1): Why PLACES adds almost zero predictive variance ($\Delta R^2_{\text{CV}} \le 0.024$) even when its raw correlation with the measured outcome is strong ($r_{\text{raw}} = 0.50\text{–}0.84$) is mathematically dictated by $1 - R^2_{P \mid X} \approx 2\%$.
By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, the in-sample $R^2$ gain from adding PLACES ($P$) to the demographic-plus-state baseline ($X$) is:
$$\Delta R^2 = R^2_{Y \sim X + P} - R^2_{Y \sim X} = r_{\text{partial}}^2 \left(1 - R^2_{Y \sim X}\right)$$
where $r_{\text{partial}} = \text{Corr}(e_{Y \mid X}, e_{P \mid X})$ is the correlation between the unexplained outcome residual $e_{Y \mid X}$ and the unexplained PLACES residual $e_{P \mid X}$. Because CDC's Multilevel Regression and Poststratification (MRP) pipeline builds $P$ from individual age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, county poverty (`<150% FPL`), and state random effects, regressing $P$ on $X$ leaves only $1 - R^2_{P \mid X} \approx 1\%\text{–}4\%$ of the variance of $P$ in $e_{P \mid X}$ (`median 2.1%` across our 21 pairs; mainly the heavily shrunk BRFSS county random effect $\hat{v}_j$ plus logistic link curvature). Even in larger counties where the BRFSS county random effect $\hat{v}_j$ is based on enough respondents to achieve a meaningful partial correlation with measured outcomes—such as Medicare hypertension ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.252$), hyperlipidemia ($0.227$), diabetes ($0.202$), asthma ($0.183$), COPD ($0.160$), ACS mobility ($0.195$), and COPD mortality ($0.205$)—squaring $r_{\text{partial}} \approx 0.20$ gives $r_{\text{partial}}^2 = 0.04$, and multiplying by $1 - R^2_{Y \mid X} \approx 0.20\text{–}0.40$ restricts $\Delta R^2$ to **0.8 to 1.7 percentage points**. Thus, PLACES's small incremental $R^2$ does not mean the BRFSS county random effect has zero validity in large counties; it means $\hat{v}_j$ is already so heavily shrunk in MRP, and county demographics plus state already capture so much of the geographic gradient ($55\%\text{–}81\%$ in large counties), that adding PLACES changes county predictions by less than $1\%\text{–}2\%$ of variance.

### Finding 2 (Rank 2): Population mismatch (Medicare 65+ FFS vs. Adults 18+) and Medicare Advantage selection explain the weak raw correlations for several Medicare conditions.
CMS Mapping Medicare Disparities (`mmd_prevalence_2023.csv`) covers **Original Medicare (Fee-For-Service) beneficiaries**, roughly 88% of whom are aged $\ge 65$ (and 12% under 65 with permanent disabilities or ESRD), whereas PLACES models **all resident adults aged $\ge 18$**. Two mechanisms distort county rankings between the two sources:
1. **Age-gradient reversals between young/middle-aged adults and seniors:** In BRFSS (`18+`), self-reported current asthma (`casthma`) and depression (`depression`) are substantially higher among younger adults (especially young women) than among seniors aged $\ge 65$, whereas in Original Medicare claims, `asthma` and `depression` reflect geriatric or disability-entitled FFS beneficiaries. Consequently, PLACES `casthma_adjprev` has **zero raw correlation** with Medicare `asthma` ($r_{\text{raw}} = 0.061$ all, $-0.023$ larger), yet once county age shares and state fixed effects are removed, their within-state partial correlation becomes positive ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.183$ in larger counties, $\Delta R^2_{\text{CV}} = +0.0242$).
2. **Medicare Advantage (MA) selection bias:** Original Medicare claims exclude beneficiaries enrolled in Medicare Advantage plans. Across the 3,129 counties with non-suppressed counts in `medicare_gv_county_2023.csv`, $\text{MA share} = \text{MA}/(\text{OM}+\text{MA})$ spans **0.0% to 81.3%** (median **45.8%**, IQR **33.6%–56.2%**). Because MA plans historically enrolled healthier beneficiaries in some markets and heavily penetrated specific urban/low-income markets in others, the remaining Original Medicare pool is a non-random, county-varying subset of seniors. Controlling for linear `ma_share` is essential (and appropriately included in Task 2), but linear `ma_share` only partially corrects for differential risk selection between FFS and MA across states and urban-rural strata.

### Finding 3 (Rank 3): Claims prevalence reflects healthcare access, diagnostic coding intensity, and survival rather than pure underlying disease prevalence.
Even within Original Medicare, a county's claims-based prevalence in MMD requires that a beneficiary (a) visit a clinician, (b) undergo diagnostic testing or screening, and (c) receive the specific ICD-10 diagnosis code on an inpatient or outpatient claim during the reference window:
- **Active treatment claims vs. lifetime self-reported history (`cancer_4_sites` vs. `cancer`, $r_{\text{raw}} = 0.085$):** MMD `cancer_4_sites` measures active 2023 Medicare claims for four specific cancers (breast, colorectal, lung, prostate), whereas PLACES `cancer_adjprev` measures whether an adult 18+ has *ever* been told they had *any* form of cancer (excluding skin cancer), which is heavily driven by long-term survivorship and screening intensity in affluent counties.
- **Asymptomatic screening and coding intensity (`hyperlipidemia`, `obesity`, `arthritis`, `ischemic_heart_disease`):** In clinical claims, `obesity` (ICD-10 `E66`) is famously under-coded on routine outpatient bills unless bariatric or metabolic treatment is billed ($r_{\text{raw}} = 0.358$ all, $0.431$ large; partial $r = 0.058$ large), even though `obesity_adjprev` has the largest non-demographic BRFSS variance in PLACES ($1 - R^2_{P \mid X} = 18.5\%$). Similarly, `hyperlipidemia` (`highchol`) and `ischemic_heart_disease` (`chd`) claims depend strongly on local primary-care/cardiology visit frequency and statin/diagnostic workup intensity (Finkelstein et al. 2016; Song et al. 2010). Thus, "Observed minus Expected" on Medicare claims maps **diagnosed and billed prevalence net of demographics**, combining true local morbidity with local practice style and care access.

### Finding 4 (Rank 4): Mortality SMRs reflect incidence $\times$ case fatality, care quality, and death-certificate coding habits rather than disease prevalence alone.
Comparing 2018–2024 WONDER cause-specific SMRs against PLACES prevalence (`diabetes`, `chd`, `stroke`, `copd`) tests whether model-based self-reported prevalence predicts cumulative mortality per age-expected death. Three factors separate mortality SMRs from prevalence:
1. **Case fatality and acute/chronic care quality:** County mortality rates equal $\text{Prevalence} \times \text{Case Fatality Rate}$ (or $\text{Incidence} \times \text{Case Fatality}$). For `heart_disease` (`GR113-054`) and `stroke` (`GR113-070`), survival depends heavily on emergency medical services (EMS) response times, proximity to primary stroke centers and cardiac catheterization labs, and secondary prevention (antihypertensives, statins, anticoagulants). Once county demographics and state fixed effects are controlled for in larger counties ($E_i \ge 100$), PLACES `chd_adjprev` and `stroke_adjprev` have near-zero partial correlation with heart disease SMR ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.008$, $\Delta R^2_{\text{CV}} = -0.0002$) and stroke SMR ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.062$, $\Delta R^2_{\text{CV}} = +0.0010$).
2. **Death-certificate underlying-cause coding variation (especially for Diabetes `GR113-046`):** WONDER `county_cause_2018_2024.txt` tabulates **Underlying Cause of Death (UCD)**. Among decedents with diabetes who die of myocardial infarction, heart failure, or stroke, whether the certifying physician or coroner lists diabetes (`E10–E14`) vs. heart disease (`I20–I51`) on the bottom line of Part I of the death certificate varies substantially across states, health systems, and rural coroners (which is why county diabetes SMRs have twice the between-county variance of heart disease or stroke SMRs: $\hat{\tau}^2 = 0.367$ vs. $0.106$ and $0.083$, yet demographic + state $R^2_{\text{CV}}$ is only $0.439$ even in $E_i \ge 100$ counties).
3. **Why COPD/CLRD (`GR113-082`) shows the strongest mortality link ($r_{\text{raw}} = 0.754$, $r_{\text{partial}} = 0.205$, $\Delta R^2_{\text{CV}} = +0.0107$ in larger counties):** Both COPD prevalence and CLRD mortality are driven by cumulative multi-decade cigarette smoking and occupational dust/coal exposure (Appalachia, Ozarks, rural industrial belt), which is partially captured by BRFSS county/state smoking history beyond standard ACS demographic covariates.

### Finding 6 (Rank 6): Shared ACS 5-year household survey sample between `acs_disability_2023.csv` and `acs_county_2023.csv` induces correlated sampling errors in small counties, and crude-vs-age-adjusted alignment matters for univariate comparisons.
1. **Shared survey sample:** Both `acs_disability_2023.csv` (ACS 2019–2023 disability by type, adults 18+) and `acs_county_2023.csv` (ACS 2019–2023 age, sex, race, education, and poverty covariates) are derived from the **exact same 2019–2023 ACS household survey microdata sample** in each county. In small counties (where 5-year ACS sampling margins of error are large), random household sampling fluctuations in the proportion of older, lower-education, or below-150%-FPL respondents sampled in county $i$ simultaneously affect both the ACS covariate columns and the ACS disability outcome columns (`acs_hearing`, `acs_mobility`, etc.). Meanwhile, PLACES disability estimates are poststratified to **Census Vintage 2023** population controls rather than the 5-year ACS sample. Thus, in `observed ~ ACS demographics + state`, shared ACS survey sampling error slightly inflates the apparent advantage of ACS covariates over PLACES, while errors-in-variables in the ACS covariates attenuate regression slopes in small counties (contributing to the jump in median `cv_r2_demo` from `0.472` across all 2,956 counties to `0.634` in the 1,485 counties with $\ge 20,000$ adults).
2. **Crude vs. age-adjusted alignment:** Because `acs_disability_2023.csv` is a **crude 18+ rate** (not age-standardized), comparing it univariately against `PLACES _adjprev` understates how well PLACES tracks ACS disability (`cv_r2_places` median `0.296` all / `0.415` larger with `_adjprev` vs. `0.353` all / `0.509` larger with `_crudeprev`, and `0.706` for `mobility_crudeprev` in larger counties), because disability rises steeply with age and `_crudeprev` retains the county's age structure. Once county age shares are controlled for in `observed ~ demographics + state + PLACES`, `_crudeprev` and `_adjprev` yield virtually identical CV $R^2$ (`0.636` median in larger counties) and negligible gain (`+0.0010` to `+0.0023`).

### Finding 7 (Rank 7): Including 50 state fixed effects inside "Expected" redefines the atlas from a national demographic expectation to a within-state anomaly map.
In all 21 models in `expected_vs_observed.py`, "Expected" is defined as $\hat{Y}_i = X_{\text{demo}, i}'\hat{\beta} + \hat{\alpha}_{s(i)}$ (plus $\hat{\gamma}\,\text{MA\_share}_i$ for Medicare), which includes **state fixed effects** $\hat{\alpha}_s$. While including state fixed effects is appropriate for testing whether PLACES adds *within-state* county signal (since PLACES itself includes a state random intercept $\hat{u}_s$), including state fixed effects in the **atlas's "Expected" baseline** has a major consequence for map interpretation:
- With state fixed effects included in "Expected", the mean residual $\overline{O - E}$ within **every single state** is forced to be **identically zero** (`0.0`). A county in Kentucky, West Virginia, or Mississippi with an average COPD mortality rate for its state will be colored **neutral (0.0 residual)** on the map, even if its mortality rate is 40% higher than a demographically identical county in Minnesota or Colorado!
- State fixed effects absorb real state-level policy, health-system, environmental, and behavioral differences (e.g., Medicaid expansion, state tobacco taxes, air quality, and state clinical coding norms), which are **not** intrinsic demographic expectations.

### Finding 8 (Rank 8): Concrete Design Recommendations for the "Expected vs. Observed" County Atlas.
To make the county atlas statistically rigorous, compliant with NCHS data-use rules, and visually honest across counties ranging from 50 residents to 10 million, we recommend the following five-part architecture:

1. **Fix the four pipeline bugs before generating the atlas layer:**
   - **Fit "Expected" on all counties with valid observed outcomes and covariates** (`N = 3,115–3,129` for Medicare, `3,144` for ACS disability, `2,909–3,118` for WONDER mortality), **never** filtering on non-missing `places_county_2025.csv` (which currently deletes all 187 counties in `KY` and `PA`, Bug 3).
   - **Mask all county-cause cells with $O_i \le 9$ (including $O_i = 0$) to `NaN`** in `build_mortality_smr.py` (`cty.loc[cty["observed"] <= 9, "observed"] = np.nan`, fixing Bug 1), and add `"02270": "02158"` to the FIPS mapping (fixing Bug 2).
   - **Use full-sample precision-weighted predictions (or repeated 10x10-fold averaged predictions) rather than single-split 10-fold CV predictions** for the map layer (fixing Bug 4 and Bug 5). In weighted least squares (WLS), weight each county by its denominator ($E_i$ for SMRs, $\text{OM}_i$ for Medicare claims, $\text{adults}_i$ for ACS disability) so that tiny counties with huge sampling noise do not distort the demographic slope estimates $\hat{\beta}$.
2. **Offer a two-mode toggle for "Expected" (National Demographic Expectation vs. Within-State Expectation):**
   - **Default Mode ("National Demographic Expectation"):** Regress the observed outcome on **county demographics only** (`age`, `sex`, `race/ethnicity`, `education`, `pov150_adults`, plus `ma_share` for Medicare claims, **without state fixed effects**). This answers the primary substantive question: *"How does this county's measured health outcome compare to a demographically and socioeconomically identical US county nationally?"*—preserving real state-level policy and regional health differences in the displayed `Observed - Expected` map.
   - **Secondary Toggle ("Within-State Expectation"):** Add state fixed effects (or empirical Bayes state random intercepts so single-county `DC` and small states `DE`, `HI`, `RI`, `VT`, `WY` are properly regularized) to highlight *local within-state* county anomalies. Do not include PLACES in "Expected" in either mode, since PLACES adds $\le 1\text{–}2\%$ variance and is missing for all of `KY` and `PA`.
3. **Apply Empirical Bayes (EB) shrinkage to `Observed - Expected` residuals so noisy small counties do not dominate the map extremes:**
   - Let $r_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i(X_i)$ be the raw residual (`Observed - Expected`) for county $i$, with known within-county sampling error variance $s_i^2$:
     - **Mortality SMRs ($\times 100$):** $s_i^2 = 100^2 \times \hat{Y}_i / (100 E_i) \approx 100^2 / E_i$ (or exact Poisson-Gamma EB posterior mean around $\hat{Y}_i$: $\tilde{Y}_i^{\text{EB}} = w_i Y_i + (1 - w_i)\hat{Y}_i$ with $w_i = E_i / (E_i + \hat{Y}_i^2 / \hat{\tau}^2)$).
     - **Medicare MMD claims (%):** $s_i^2 = \frac{\hat{p}_i(100 - \hat{p}_i)}{\text{OM}_i} + \frac{1}{12}$ (combining binomial variance on Original Medicare count $\text{OM}_i$ and integer-rounding variance $1/12$).
     - **ACS disability (%):** $s_i^2 = (\text{MOE}_{i, 90\%} / 1.645)^2$ from the ACS summary file margin-of-error columns.
   - Estimate the true between-county residual variance $\hat{\tau}^2 = \max\!\big(0,\, \text{Var}(r_i) - \overline{s_i^2}\big)$ (or via REML / moments weighted by precision), and display the **Empirical Bayes shrunk residual**:
     $$\tilde{r}_i^{\text{EB}} = \lambda_i (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i), \qquad \lambda_i = \frac{\hat{\tau}^2}{\hat{\tau}^2 + s_i^2} \in (0, 1)$$
   - In large counties ($E_i \ge 100$, $\text{OM}_i \ge 5,000$, $\text{adults}_i \ge 20,000$), $\lambda_i \approx 0.90\text{–}0.99$, so $\tilde{r}_i^{\text{EB}}$ is nearly identical to the raw residual. In tiny counties ($E_i = 5$, $\text{OM}_i = 200$), $\lambda_i$ shrinks pure noise toward zero, preventing small rural counties from monopolizing the top and bottom color deciles of the choropleth.
4. **Display standardized excess alongside raw excess (`Observed - Expected` and $z$-score / posterior probability):**
   - In tooltips and side panels, show: Observed ($Y_i$), Expected ($\hat{Y}_i$), Shrunk Excess ($\tilde{r}_i^{\text{EB}}$ in percentage points or SMR points, with 95% credible interval), and the shrinkage reliability weight $\lambda_i$.
5. **Enforce strict suppression and three-tier reliability flags:**
   - **Hard Suppression (Never Displayed):**
     - **CDC WONDER:** Any county-cause cell with $O_i \le 9$ (including both WONDER `"Suppressed"` cells $1\text{–}9$ and zero-death cells $O_i = 0$, or any county with expected deaths $E_i < 10$) must be suppressed (`NaN`, gray hatched fill labeled *"Suppressed: $\le 9$ deaths or $<10$ expected"*), and suppressed counts must never appear in tooltips, tables, or downloadable CSVs.
     - **CMS Medicare (`mmd_prevalence_2023.csv` / `medicare_gv_county_2023.csv`):** Any county with MMD `rate == 0` or `BENES_OM_CNT == '*'` / `BENES_MA_CNT == '*'` must be masked as CMS-suppressed.
   - **Reliability Tiers on the Map:**
     - **Tier 1 (High Reliability, solid fill):** $\lambda_i \ge 0.75$ (roughly $E_i \ge 50\text{–}100$ deaths, $\text{OM}_i \ge 2,500\text{–}5,000$, or $\text{adults}_i \ge 10,000\text{–}20,000$).
     - **Tier 2 (Moderate Reliability / Heavily Shrunk, muted or stippled fill + warning badge in tooltip):** $0.40 \le \lambda_i < 0.75$ (e.g., $10 \le E_i < 50$ or $500 \le \text{OM}_i < 2,500$).
     - **Tier 3 (Unreliable / Low Denominator, suppressed or grayed out by default):** $\lambda_i < 0.40$ or $E_i < 10$ or $\text{OM}_i < 500$.

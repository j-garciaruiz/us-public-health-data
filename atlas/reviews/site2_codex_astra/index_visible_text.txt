Demographics and state reproduce most of the variation in PLACES

Across 40 crude-prevalence measures, county demographics plus state indicators reproduce a median 96.2% of the variation in counties the model did not see. This approximates the inputs to PLACES; it does not rerun CDC's model or show that the underlying health differences are artificial. Uninsurance measured directly by the Census is less predictable, partly because of sampling error.

Once demographics and state are known, PLACES adds little

In 21 measured outcomes (deaths, Medicare diagnoses and Census disability), demographics plus state, and Medicare Advantage share for Medicare, predict each outcome better than PLACES alone. Adding PLACES raises prediction accuracy on unseen counties by at most 0.8 points across all counties (shown below) and 2.3 points in larger counties.

What the atlas shows

Differences are descriptive comparisons with a model, not estimates of preventable disease or county performance.

452 vs 145

Diabetes deaths in Caddo Parish, Louisiana

Diabetes deaths run at 4.5 times the national rate for Caddo's age mix (an index of 452, where 100 is the national rate), against 145 expected from its demographics. Iberia Parish, elsewhere in Louisiana, has an index of 38 against 166 expected. The contrast could reflect differences in disease, care, how deaths are certified or other factors; this analysis does not distinguish them.

44% vs 28%

Recorded diabetes in Staten Island

Share of Original Medicare beneficiaries with a recorded diabetes diagnosis, against the share expected from county demographics and Medicare Advantage enrollment. Brooklyn: 43% against 29%. Disease, access to care, who is in Original Medicare, and diagnosis or coding practices may all contribute; this analysis does not separate them.

Using PLACES with its limitations
Suited to
Estimating likely burden where no local data exist (CDC's intended use)
Planning and needs assessments
Broad geographic patterns
Not suited to, on its own
Judging whether a county does better or worse than similar counties
Evaluating a local program or policy
Treating links with demographics, or between two PLACES measures, as independent evidence
Treating modeled differences as precise rankings of county health

Prefer direct local estimates when they measure the same outcome, population and period with enough precision. Claims and death records add complementary evidence, but they do not measure the same thing as adult prevalence surveys.

How it was checked

Independent reviews by Codex and Agy reconstructed specified analyses and checked their methods. Their findings led to corrections in county matching, suppression, prediction and smoothing; the review reports describe each review's scope and the remaining limits.

Build

Analysis written by Claude (Opus 5.5) under the author's direction, from public raw files.

Replicate

Codex (GPT-6 Astra) reconstructed the analyses within each review's stated scope, then audited the code.

Challenge

Agy (Gemini 4 Argon) reviewed the method and recomputed headline numbers. Its findings were verified before use.

Correct

Documented problems were corrected; unresolved limitations are listed below.

Problems the reviews caught
Correlations between two measures computed on mismatched sets of counties
A summary figure that could not exceed 1.9% even if two measures moved together perfectly
Zero-death counties kept in the data, against the CDC rule on counts of 9 or fewer
A renamed Alaska county dropped from the mortality data
Kentucky and Pennsylvania missing from every atlas layer
Negative expected death rates in four counties
A small-county smoothing formula missing a centering step
Wording that claimed more than the analysis showed, on charts and on this page
Method, data and limits
Data
CDC PLACES, 2025 county release (BRFSS 2022-2023)
CDC WONDER deaths 2018-2024, under the NCHS data use rules
CMS Mapping Medicare Disparities, 2023 claims
Census American Community Survey, 2019-2023
Method
Expected values: predicted from county demographics (plus Medicare Advantage share for claims, and optionally state) by models fit on other counties, so no county predicts itself; deaths use a rate model (Poisson regression)
Deaths: compared with the deaths expected at national age-specific rates (standardized mortality ratio, 100 = national rate)
Smoothing: pulls noisy small-county differences toward zero according to each source's uncertainty (empirical Bayes); Medicare denominators are approximate, and age-standardized Medicare is not smoothed
Limits
Measured is not the same as true disease: claims reflect diagnosis and coding, deaths reflect certificates
Connecticut has no mortality match; Medicare smoothing uses an approximate denominator
No test yet on other regions or years

Builds on Kong and Zhang, "The Use of Small Area Estimates in Place-Based Health Research", American Journal of Public Health, 2020.
